2. The Problem, Explained From Zero
2.1 What is ocean temperature, and why does it change with depth?

Water at the ocean surface is heated by the sun and warmed/cooled by contact with the atmosphere. That heat only slowly mixes downward
because water is a poor conductor and because warm water is less dense than cold water — it floats on top. So a vertical slice of ocean
typically looks like:
Mixed layer (surface to ~20–100 m): wind and waves keep this layer well-stirred, so temperature is nearly uniform with depth.
Thermocline (below the mixed layer): temperature drops sharply over a relatively thin band — this is the ocean's "temperature cliff."
Deep ocean (below ~1000 m): cold (near 2–4°C) and nearly uniform, fed by polar water that sank long ago and slowly spreads along
the bottom.
Analogy: think of a glass of iced coffee left still. The top warms in the sun, the bottom stays cold near the ice, and there's a thin zone in
between where temperature changes fast. Stir it (wind) and the warm zone thickens; stop stirring and the sharp transition reappears.
2.2 Why do we care what's happening below the surface?

Not a list — a causal chain, worked through for the flagship example:
How does a temperature profile affect cyclone intensity? A cyclone draws its energy from evaporating warm surface water. As wind
churns the sea, it pulls up water from just below the surface (upwelling/mixing). If the thermocline is deep and the subsurface water is
nearly as warm as the surface (high Tropical Cyclone Heat Potential), the churned-up water is still warm, so the storm keeps getting fed
and can intensify. If the thermocline is shallow, churning drags up cold water, the surface cools, and the storm's fuel supply is cut — this is
one reason cyclones sometimes stall or weaken unexpectedly even over "warm" seas. You cannot get this right from SST alone — you need
to know how warm it stays as you go down. This is the mechanistic reason INCOIS (which issues cyclone guidance for India) wants
subsurface temperature, not just SST.

Similarly:
Marine heatwaves are now defined and tracked at depth, not just at the surface, because subsurface heat has separate ecological and
fisheries consequences (species displacement, coral stress at depth, changed fish migration).
Fisheries: many commercially important species track the thermocline depth (their preferred temperature band), so thermocline
depth maps directly inform fishing-ground forecasts (a related SIH-adjacent application area, e.g. Potential Fishing Zone advisories).
Ocean circulation / stratification: the vertical density structure (driven by temperature and salinity) sets up the pressure gradients
that drive currents; a bias in subsurface temperature propagates into circulation forecasts.
Data assimilation / forecasting: operational ocean forecast systems (like GLORYS's production system) need frequent estimates of
the full 3-D state to correct their models; a fast statistical proxy for the 3-D field is useful as a first guess or independent check.

Core physical answer to "why can't satellites just measure subsurface temperature?" Because heat and light from the ocean interior are
almost completely absorbed or scattered within the top few millimeters to centimeters of water. A satellite radiometer literally cannot
receive a signal that originated at 200 m depth. What satellites can see is the surface expression of processes whose causes and effects
extend into the interior:
Thermocline displacement / mesoscale eddies: a warm-core eddy bulges the sea surface upward (measurable by altimetry as +SSH)
and pushes the thermocline deeper; a cold-core eddy does the opposite. So SSH is a statistical proxy for thermocline depth.
Vertical mixing: strong winds (scatterometer-measured) mix the upper layers, which is visible as SST cooling and is also felt at depth.
Geostrophic transport: SSH gradients drive currents (via geostrophic balance), and currents advect subsurface water masses — so
surface currents carry information about what water is "downstream."
Air-sea coupling: heat fluxes that set SST also set the depth and strength of the mixed layer.

None of these links are exact or one-to-one — that's exactly why this is a statistical/ML learning problem rather than a simple formula, and
exactly why a reanalysis product with real subsurface physics (GLORYS) is needed to teach the model the actual (model-consistent)
relationship.

5. Datasets — What They Actually Are
5.1 GLORYS (training target)
[D/A] GLORYS12 (the current flagship version, "GLORYS12V1") is produced by Mercator Ocean International for the EU's Copernicus
Marine Service (CMEMS). It is not a satellite product and not direct observation — it is the output of a physical ocean circulation model
(NEMO), forced by atmospheric reanalysis (historically ERA-Interim, more recent versions use ERA5-class forcing), into which real
observations are assimilated: along-track altimeter sea level anomaly, satellite SST, sea-ice concentration, and in-situ temperature/salinity
profiles (including ARGO), corrected via a reduced-order Kalman filter plus a 3D-VAR large-scale bias correction. Native resolution is 1/12°
(~8 km) with 50 vertical levels from the surface to ~5500 m, covering 1993–present. GLORYS has been extensively validated and is
considered one of the strongest global ocean reanalyses in comparative studies, though it still has known regional biases, especially where
in-situ observation density (which corrects the model) is historically low — which includes parts of the North Indian Ocean before the
ARGO era matured.
Ground truth vs. reanalysis target — do not conflate these. "Ground truth" means direct, calibrated physical measurement (an ARGO
CTD sensor reading). GLORYS is a model estimate constrained by observations — it inherits real observations where they exist but fills in
the rest using ocean-model physics, which has its own biases (mixing schemes, resolution limits, forcing errors). If your model learns
purely from GLORYS, in the strictest sense it learns "what GLORYS's model+data-assimilation system would predict," not "the true
ocean." This is exactly why the PS mandates independent ARGO validation — a model that matches GLORYS extremely well but matches
independent ARGO poorly has learned GLORYS's biases, not ocean physics. Report both.
5.2 ARGO (independent validation)
[D/A] ARGO is an international array of ~4000 autonomous profiling floats. Each float drifts at a "parking depth" (usually ~1000 m), then
every ~10 days descends to ~2000 m and rises to the surface, measuring temperature, salinity, and pressure continuously on the way up,
then transmits the profile via satellite before sinking again. This gives high-accuracy, full-depth (to 2000 m) profiles, but at any given time
and 0.25° grid cell, the odds of having a float physically present are low — which is precisely why ARGO alone cannot give you a
continuous daily gridded field, but is exactly trustworthy enough to be the gold-standard check on a model.
INCOIS's Live Access Server (LAS) provides a gridded ARGO product for the Indian Ocean — i.e., ARGO's sparse profiles have already
been objectively interpolated onto a regular grid by INCOIS. [gap] The exact resolution/depth levels/methodology of this specific INCOIS
gridded ARGO product should be confirmed from the LAS documentation directly (search "INCOIS LAS gridded Argo") since it may differ

from GLORYS's depths and the PS's 15 standard levels. A general Indian-Ocean ARGO SST/gridded climatology paper found in our
literature search reports 0.25°×0.25° monthly gridded ARGO products constructed using the DIVA (Data Interpolating Variational
Analysis) interpolation method — a similar approach is plausible for the LAS product but must be verified against the actual LAS
metadata, not assumed.
Training vs validation, intuitively: imagine studying for an exam using a detailed answer key (GLORYS) that was itself written by a very
good but imperfect teacher. Your "validation" should be a different, independently graded test (ARGO) — if you only check your answers
against the same answer key you studied from, you'll always look perfect, even if you only memorized quirks of that particular teacher
rather than the actual subject.

8. Remote Sensing Fundamentals (compressed)
[D] Passive sensors (infrared/microwave radiometers) measure naturally emitted radiation — used for SST. Active sensors (altimeters,
scatterometers) transmit and measure the return signal — used for SSH (radar altimetry) and winds (scatterometry, via sea-surface
roughness). Raw satellite data usually arrives as swath (L1/L2) data tied to the orbit track; operational users normally consume gridded
(L3/L4) products where multiple satellites/passes have been merged and, for L4, gap-filled by interpolation — you should use L4equivalent, already-gridded products (CMEMS SST L4/OSTIA, DUACS SSH L4) rather than raw swaths, both because the PS demands a
uniform daily 0.25° grid and because building your own swath-to-grid pipeline is out of scope for a hackathon timeline. Clouds block
infrared SST retrieval (not microwave), which is why blended L4 products exist — they use microwave + statistical interpolation to cover IR
gaps.

9. Scientific Data Engineering
Formats: NetCDF (self-describing gridded scientific data, the de facto standard here), Zarr (chunked, cloud-friendly, good for large training
sets), read via xarray (labelled multi-dimensional arrays; makes lat/lon/time-aware code readable) with Dask for out-of-core/parallel
processing when data won't fit in RAM. CDO (Climate Data Operators) is a fast command-line tool for regridding/remapping before you
even touch Python, often the pragmatic first step.
Regridding choices: bilinear (fast, smooths fields, fine for continuous fields like SST/SSH), conservative remapping (preserves areaintegrated quantities, better when going from fine to coarse grids and when mass/heat conservation matters scientifically), nearestneighbour (only for masks/categorical data, avoid for continuous physical fields).
Concrete answer to "six datasets, six grids, one ML-ready dataset":

1. Fix a common analysis grid: 0.25°×0.25°, lat 5–30°N, lon 45–105°E, daily timestamps (this is the target grid, since GLORYS/ARGO/PS
already specify it).
2. For each source dataset: inspect native grid/CRS/units/calendar → regrid spatially (bilinear for SST/SSH/currents/winds; consider
conservative if downsampling from very fine native SST) → resample temporally to daily (mean for sub-daily sources like ERA5
winds; nearest/linear-in-time for already-daily products) → convert units → rename coordinates to a shared convention ( lat , lon ,
time , depth ).

3. Apply a shared land/ocean mask (derived from GLORYS bathymetry, since that's your target grid) to every input and output field so
land pixels don't pollute loss computation.
4. Stack all variables into one aligned xarray Dataset on the common grid/time axis; write to Zarr chunked by time for efficient randomaccess training reads.
5. Only after this alignment step do you do train/val/test splitting and tensor generation (Part 10).
Dangers: regridding before unit conversion can silently corrupt values (e.g., interpolating raw Kelvin vs Celsius is fine, but interpolating
across a bad fill-value like -32768 without masking first will destroy the field). Never interpolate over land into ocean cells or vice versa
without a correct mask — this leaks unphysical values into your training data.

11. What "Embedding" Actually Means Here
An embedding is a way of compressing a large, structured input into a smaller vector (or smaller feature map) that still preserves the
information that matters for the task, while discarding irrelevant noise. Analogy: instead of describing a person's face pixel by pixel, you
describe them by a handful of learned traits (face shape, eye spacing, etc.) — that's an embedding of the face.
In an autoencoder, an encoder network compresses input → latent vector (the embedding); a decoder reconstructs something from that
latent vector. In representation learning more broadly, you don't need to reconstruct the original input — you can decode to a different
target, which is exactly OceanEmbed's setup:
Surface fields [7 channels, H, W]
↓

↓

← this IS "the satellite embedding"

Temperature Profile [15 depths, H, W]

Why this helps: the raw 7-channel surface state is high-dimensional and noisy; the encoder is forced to learn a compact representation that
only keeps information useful for reconstructing structure that correlates with subsurface temperature (eddy presence/sign, mixed-layer

depth signal, etc.), which acts as a form of regularization and also gives you an inspectable, visualizable intermediate product for your demo
("here is what the model learned to notice about the ocean surface before it even looks at depth").

13. Baselines (mandatory)
Before any deep model, establish:
1. Climatology: predict the long-term mean temperature-at-depth for that day-of-year and location (from GLORYS training years).
Answers "how much better than just knowing the season are we?"
2. Persistence: predict "same as N days ago" (less meaningful here since target is depth structure, but useful for later spatiotemporal
extensions).
3. Linear regression / Ridge: per-depth linear regression from the 7 surface channels (globally or per grid cell).
4. Random Forest / XGBoost: nonlinear tabular baseline using surface channels (+ lat/lon/day-of-year) as features, trained per depth
level.
5. Simple MLP: shared small MLP applied per-pixel.
6. Simple CNN: shallow 3–4 layer CNN, no fancy embedding stage.
Why mandatory: if your U-Net gets RMSE = 0.8°C at 100 m, that number is meaningless in isolation. If climatology alone already gets
RMSE = 0.9°C at that depth (very plausible near the seasonal thermocline, which is fairly predictable), your "advanced" model is only
marginally useful — and a judge asking "why deep learning?" deserves that comparison, not a vague appeal to sophistication.

15. Literature Synthesis
What's already solved: the basic feasibility question — "can surface satellite fields predict subsurface temperature with useful skill using
deep learning" — is well-established in the literature (multiple independent groups, multiple ocean basins, multiple architectures from
linear regression through ConvLSTM to graph attention networks, spanning 2022–2026). This is not novel research territory in the
abstract.
What remains genuinely open / regionally specific: a North Indian Ocean-specific, 0.25°/daily, 15-standard-depth, GLORYS-trainedARGO-validated implementation targeted specifically at INCOIS's operational depth levels and domain does not appear to be a publicly
available, off-the-shelf product — most published work targets the Pacific, South China Sea, or global scale at different depth/resolution
choices. That regional/operational specificity is your realistic contribution (see Part 18/Novelty).
Consistent findings across papers (general pattern, not all individually re-verified here): skill (correlation, RMSE) is typically highest
near the surface and mixed layer, degrades through the thermocline (roughly 100–300 m, where vertical gradients are steep and small
depth errors translate into large temperature errors), and often improves again below ~500–700 m where the ocean is more uniform
and slowly varying. This U-shaped (or "worst-in-the-middle") error-vs-depth pattern is a very defensible, literature-consistent expectation
to state to judges rather than a specific number.
Role of Argo vs reanalysis, restated: reanalysis (GLORYS) supplies dense, gap-free training targets everywhere; ARGO supplies the
sparser but independently-trustworthy check. Multiple papers explicitly combine both, exactly as this PS mandates.
Realistic research gap for a six-person student team: not a new architecture, but a well-executed, honestly-validated, INCOISoperational-parameter-matched regional implementation with a clean demo — and, if time allows, a genuine (if modest) empirical question
such as "does adding surface currents (U,V) meaningfully improve skill over SST+SSH+SSS alone in the Bay of Bengal specifically?" — this
is answerable in a hackathon timeframe via an ablation study and is a legitimate, evidence-backed claim of contribution.

17. Loss Functions
MSE: standard, penalizes large errors heavily — reasonable default.
MAE: more robust to outliers (e.g., rare extreme ARGO or GLORYS values); good complement metric even if not the training loss.
Huber: middle ground, good practical default for noisy geophysical targets.
Depth-weighted loss: should 5 m and 1000 m be weighted equally? [E] No, and here's the reasoning, not an assumption: the
standard depths are not evenly spaced (5 m steps near the surface, 200–300 m steps below 500 m) and the physical variability of
temperature is far larger and more dynamically important near the surface/thermocline than in the deep, nearly-isothermal ocean.
Two defensible weighting choices: (a) inverse-variance weighting (weight each depth by 1/GLORYS-climatological-variance at that
depth, so the loss doesn't get dominated by the naturally larger swings near the surface), or (b) uniform weighting but always report
depth-resolved metrics separately so no single number hides thermocline-region failure. [E] Recommendation: train with uniform
or mildly variance-weighted loss, but never report a single averaged RMSE as the headline metric — always show the depth-wise
breakdown.

18. Evaluation Plan
Report, at minimum: RMSE, MAE, Bias, Pearson correlation, and (where sample size allows) R², each broken down by depth, season, and
sub-region (Arabian Sea vs Bay of Bengal) — never a single overall number as the headline result.
Proposed plots: predicted-vs-observed profile at sample ARGO locations; depth-vs-RMSE curve; depth-vs-bias curve; depth-vs-correlation
curve; spatial error maps per depth; predicted temperature maps vs GLORYS/ARGO maps side by side; vertical cross-sections along a
chosen latitude/longitude line. Taylor diagrams are a nice-to-have if time allows (they compactly show correlation + variance ratio + RMSE
together, and judges familiar with atmospheric/ocean science recognize them immediately, which helps credibility).

19. Physical Sanity Checks
[E] Recommendation, with cost/benefit stated rather than assumed: don't build a full physics-informed loss function (high complexity,
uncertain payoff in the timeline) — instead add a cheap, high-value post-hoc physical plausibility check: verify the predicted profile is
monotonically non-increasing with depth below the mixed layer for the vast majority of grid points/days (temperature inversions at depth
are physically rare in this domain outside specific known phenomena), and flag/report the fraction of predictions that violate this. This
costs almost nothing to implement, gives you a genuinely impressive judge-facing plot ("<X% of our predictions show unphysical
inversions"), and demonstrates scientific rigor without committing engineering time to a custom physics-informed loss.

20. Uncertainty
[E] Recommendation: full probabilistic modelling (quantile regression, MC-dropout ensembles, CRPS scoring) is valuable but likely too
costly for the base timeline. Propose it explicitly as the "advanced" tier: a simple, cheap proxy is MC-Dropout at inference time (keep
dropout active, run N forward passes, report the spread as an uncertainty estimate) — this requires zero architecture change, only
inference-time behavior, making it a very good "if time permits" addition rather than an MVP requirement.

21. Final Architecture Recommendation
Architecture A — simplest viable (fallback / guaranteed to finish): shallow CNN encoder-decoder, no fancy embedding visualization,
MSE loss, single depth-averaged skill report. Use this if the team is badly behind schedule.
Architecture B — recommended: U-Net-style encoder-decoder with an explicit bottleneck latent layer (this is your "embedding"), Huber
loss with depth-aware weighting, full depth/season/region-resolved evaluation, monotonicity sanity check, MC-Dropout uncertainty as a
bonus if time allows. [E] This is our top recommendation — it satisfies every explicit PS requirement, is buildable and trainable within a
hackathon compute budget (laptop GPU or free-tier Colab), and gives you a clean, visualizable embedding to show judges.
Architecture C — ambitious: Architecture B's U-Net backbone with a lightweight self-attention block at the bottleneck (cheap globalcontext boost, avoids full ViT data-hunger), plus a ConvLSTM temporal extension trained on a short sequence window if the team has
bandwidth in the final days, plus the ablation study (does adding currents help?) as the "genuine contribution" claim.
Why not go straight to full ViT or GNN? Both explicitly appear as PS-suggested options, so mentioning them is legitimate — but both are
data-hungry or engineering-heavy relative to the domain size (~24,000 grid cells) and hackathon timeline; a U-Net gets you comparable or
better practical skill at a fraction of the implementation risk, which is the correct engineering trade-off to defend to judges.

22. System Architecture, Stack, and Compute
Layers: Data layer (raw + harmonized Zarr store) → Processing layer (regridding/normalization scripts) → ML layer (PyTorch training/eval
code) → Model serving layer (FastAPI wrapping the trained model) → Frontend (map + profile viewer) — keep the research/ML pipeline
(notebooks, training scripts, evaluation) architecturally separate from the demo/application layer (API + frontend) so judges can inspect
either independently, and so a frontend bug never blocks the ML story.
Stack [E], each justified:
Python — ecosystem requirement, no real alternative for this domain.
PyTorch over TensorFlow — better ecosystem fit for quick iteration and easier custom loss/masking logic for a small team; either is
defensible, PyTorch is our pick for familiarity/speed of hackathon iteration.
xarray + NumPy + Dask — necessary for correct, labelled multi-dimensional geoscience data handling; Dask only if a laptop can't
hold the harmonized dataset in memory (if it fits, skip Dask to save complexity/time).
CDO or xESMF for regridding — faster/more reliable than hand-rolled interpolation.
NetCDF4/Zarr — required scientific data formats.
Cartopy + Matplotlib for scientific maps; Plotly only if you want interactive frontend charts — don't build both, pick one per surface.
FastAPI for a minimal inference API — lightweight, fast to stand up.
A simple static or lightweight React frontend — only as complex as needed to show a map + a click-to-see-profile interaction; do not
over-invest here relative to the ML story.
Docker — optional but good practice for reproducibility; skip if it threatens the timeline.
GitHub — required for team collaboration and judge-visible history.
Compute: domain is small (~100×240 cells, 15 depths, 7 input channels) — a free-tier Colab/Kaggle GPU (T4-class, ~16 GB VRAM) is
more than sufficient for Architecture A/B; storage for several years of harmonized daily 0.25° data over this domain is on the order of a
few GB to low tens of GB depending on year range chosen, not TBs — [E] estimate, not benchmarked; verify against your actual
downloaded data size early.

28. Judge Cross-Questioning (representative set — expand with your own team's rehearsal)
(Short answer / deep answer / what not to say, for each.)

1. Why deep learning instead of a simpler statistical method? Short: Because the surface-to-subsurface relationship is nonlinear and
spatially structured; our baselines (linear regression, RF) quantify exactly how much DL gains us. Deep: Linear methods can't capture
eddy-dependent, spatially-varying relationships; our ablation shows DL beats RF by X (report your actual number). Don't say:
"Because deep learning is state of the art" with no baseline comparison — this is exactly the trap Part 15 exists to avoid.
2. Why GLORYS as the training target and not something else? Short: It's the officially recommended, DOI-cited dataset, and it's the
best-validated eddy-resolving global reanalysis available with full depth coverage. Deep: explain assimilation scheme (Part 5.1) and
cite its known regional strengths/limitations honestly. Don't say: "GLORYS is ground truth" — it is a model-constrained reanalysis, not
direct observation.
3. Why ARGO for validation and not just a GLORYS holdout? Short: To prove we learned real ocean physics, not GLORYS's internal
biases. Deep: explain the "answer key vs. independent exam" analogy (Part 5.2). Don't say: claim validation is "perfect" — ARGO itself
has sparse coverage and its own representativeness error at a 0.25° grid cell.
4. Why not a random train/test split? Short: Spatial/temporal autocorrelation would leak information and inflate our reported skill.
Deep: explain spatio-temporal blocked splitting (Part 16).

5. What happens below 500 m — does skill collapse? Short: Expect improved, not worse, skill deep down, because deep water is more
uniform; the hardest zone is the thermocline (~100–300 m). Deep: cite the literature-consistent U-shaped error-vs-depth pattern (Part
15), show your actual depth-vs-RMSE plot.
6. What is your genuine novelty? Short: Not a new architecture — a regional, INCOIS-parameter-matched, honestly-validated
operational pipeline plus an ablation study on which surface variables matter most in this basin. Don't say: claim to have invented a
fundamentally new method — the literature review shows this is an active, multi-group research area already.
(Continue rehearsing similarly for: scalability, security/data integrity of the pipeline, why not existing systems like existing gridded-Argo
products alone, deployment plan, handling of missing satellite data operationally, extreme-event/monsoon generalization, and
computational cost at operational (basin-wide, daily) scale.)

30. What We Can Claim
Can confidently claim (after building): "our pipeline correctly harmonizes N heterogeneous datasets to a common 0.25°/daily grid," "our
baselines establish a defensible skill floor," "our deep model beats [specific baseline] by [measured amount] at [specific depths/regions] on
independent ARGO validation," "predictions are physically plausible in >X% of cases (monotonicity check)."
Requires experimental evidence before claiming: "our model generalizes well to unseen years/regions" (only if your actual spatiotemporal holdout supports it), "surface currents meaningfully improve skill" (only after running the ablation).
Must NOT claim without evidence: "this is more accurate than existing INCOIS operational products" (you have no access to compare
against those), "this works at true real-time operational latency" (a hackathon PoC is not a production system), "this generalizes beyond the
North Indian Ocean domain trained on," "GLORYS is ground truth" (Part 5.1).

31. Novelty — Honest Assessment
[Brutally honest per the brief's own instruction]: the general capability — reconstructing subsurface ocean temperature from surface
satellite fields via deep learning, trained on reanalysis, validated on ARGO — is not novel; it is an active multi-group research area with
several published implementations across 2022–2026 spanning the Pacific, South China Sea, and global scale, using architectures from
linear regression through ConvLSTM to graph attention networks. A student team should not claim to have invented this approach.
What is realistically, defensibly novel/contributory for this project:

A North Indian Ocean-specific implementation matched exactly to INCOIS's 15 operational standard depths and 0.25°/daily
operational grid, which does not appear as an off-the-shelf public product.

An explicit, visualized "satellite embedding" bottleneck as a first-class, inspectable artifact (many published papers treat the network
as an end-to-end black box rather than foregrounding the embedding, despite the PS explicitly asking for one).
A genuine, executable ablation study (which surface variables matter most, Arabian Sea vs. Bay of Bengal) as real, small-scale original
evidence.
A rigorously honest spatio-temporal validation methodology and physical-plausibility sanity checking, presented transparently
rather than glossed over — itself a legitimate engineering contribution in a field where leakage and over-claiming are common failure
modes (see Part 29).

32. Where Our Reasoning Could Be Wrong
GLORYS itself may have systematic North Indian Ocean biases (as noted, in-situ observation density historically constrains
assimilation quality, and this region has complex monsoon-driven dynamics) — if GLORYS is biased in a particular season/region, the
model will learn and reproduce that bias, and ARGO validation, while a genuine check, is itself sparse enough in this basin that a bias
could partly evade detection. Test: explicitly stratify ARGO validation by season and sub-region and look for systematic signconsistent bias, not just aggregate RMSE.
0.25°/daily may not be fine enough to resolve the mesoscale eddies that are mechanistically central to the surface-subsurface
link (eddies can be tens of km across, near the grid's resolution limit) — if so, the "embedding" may be learning a coarser, climatologydominated relationship rather than true eddy-resolved dynamics. Test: check whether skill is meaningfully better than climatology
specifically in high-eddy-activity regions/seasons vs. low-activity ones.
The 15 fixed standard depths, chosen for their oceanographic/operational relevance, are unevenly spaced, which could bias a
naively-averaged loss toward the surface-dense depths — addressed via depth-aware weighting and always reporting per-depth
metrics (Part 17), but the specific weighting scheme chosen is itself a modeling assumption, not a proven-optimal choice; different
weightings should be quickly compared as a sensitivity check if time allows.
A U-Net's local receptive field may under-capture basin-scale monsoon-driven signals that a full ViT or GNN might capture better
— the choice of Architecture B over C/ViT is a pragmatic timeline trade-off, not a claim that U-Net is scientifically superior; this should
be stated plainly to judges rather than defended as if it were obviously correct.

33. Learning Roadmap for the Team
1. Python scientific computing basics (NumPy, Pandas) — foundation.
2. xarray + NetCDF — you cannot touch the actual data without this.
3. Basic physical oceanography (Part 7 table) — enough to speak credibly, not a full course.
4. Remote sensing basics (Part 8) — enough to justify dataset choices.
5. Geospatial regridding concepts — needed before writing the pipeline.
6. CNNs and encoder-decoder architectures — core model family.
7. Autoencoders / embeddings conceptually — needed to frame "the embedding" correctly.
8. (Optional, advanced tier only) Transformers/ViT, ConvLSTM, GNN basics.
9. Model evaluation methodology for geoscience (why not just accuracy/R² blindly; depth/season/region-resolved reporting) — critical,
often skipped by hackathon teams, a strong differentiator.
10. Scientific visualization (Cartopy/Matplotlib basics for maps).
11. Minimal API/deployment (FastAPI) — enough to serve a model, not a full backend course.
What can be skipped: deep numerical-ocean-modelling theory (NEMO internals), full CRS/geodesy theory beyond "know your lat/lon grid
and don't mix projections," building your own satellite retrieval algorithms (use existing L4 products).
