PART 1 — THE WHOLE PROJECT IN ONE PAGE
What we are building. Satellites can only see the skin of the ocean. We take what satellites
see at the surface, and predict what the water looks like all the way down to 1000 m —
temperature and salinity at every depth, every day, across the Bay of Bengal and Arabian Sea.
Why anyone cares. Cyclones do not feed on surface temperature. They feed on the depth of
the warm water. A storm’s winds stir the ocean like a spoon in a cup. If the warm layer is thin,
the stirring pulls up cold water from below, the surface cools, and the storm starves. If the
warm layer is deep, the stirring only brings up more warm water, and the storm explodes in
strength.
Two spots in the Bay of Bengal can both read 30 °C from space and behave completely
differently, because one has 30 m of warm water under it and the other has 100 m. A satellite
cannot tell those two spots apart. Our model can.
Why it is hard. The only direct measurements of the deep ocean come from Argo floats —
drifting robots that dive and surface. There is roughly one float per 3° square, reporting once
every 10 days. Almost the entire basin is unmeasured almost all of the time.
Why it is possible. Warm water expands. Where the warm layer is thick, the sea surface
physically bulges upward by a few centimetres. Satellite altimeters measure that bulge to
about 2 cm. So sea surface height is effectively a tape measure for how deep the warm water
goes. That one fact is the scientific foundation of the entire project.
Our name. The halocline is the layer where saltiness changes sharply with depth. In the Bay
of Bengal it is created by river water and it is the reason this basin behaves differently from
everywhere else on Earth. It is the thing our model treats specially and nobody else does.

Nine ideas. Each one is here because it turns into a line of code or a slide. Nothing else.

2.1 The ocean has three layers
Picture a cup of tea you just stirred.
• Mixed layer — the top. Wind and waves stir it, so it is the same temperature from top to
bottom. Usually 10–120 m thick.
• Thermocline — below it, temperature falls off a cliff. Roughly 50–200 m here. Almost
everything interesting happens in this layer.
• Deep ocean — below ~500 m. Cold, slow, barely connected to the surface on a day-to-day
basis.
Why we care: our model will be excellent between 20 m and 200 m, and useless at 700 m and
1000 m. That is not a bug we can fix — the information simply is not in the surface data. We
must say this openly and show it as a number.
In the code: we report a skill score at every depth separately, rather than one average error
for the whole water column. An average would hide the good and the bad in each other.

2.2 The barrier layer, and why the Bay of Bengal is special
The Ganges, Brahmaputra and Irrawaddy dump enormous amounts of fresh water into the
Bay of Bengal. Fresh water is lighter than salty water, so it floats on top, like oil on water.
That floating fresh lid is hard to mix through. Wind can stir the top few metres, but it cannot
punch down into the warm water below. The layer between “as far as stirring reaches” and
“where the temperature actually drops” is the barrier layer. It acts as a lid that protects the
warm water underneath from being disturbed.
Three definitions we actually compute:
• MLD (mixed layer depth) — how deep the density stays roughly constant. Stirring reaches
this far.
• ILD (isothermal layer depth) — how deep the temperature stays roughly constant.
• BLT (barrier layer thickness) = ILD − MLD. When this is large, there is a protected warm
layer, and a cyclone over it can intensify without limit.
The number that makes the case: in seawater, 1 unit of saltiness is worth about 3 °C in
terms of how heavy the water is. So in the Bay of Bengal, saltiness controls the layering,
not temperature. Almost every global model underweights this. We do not.

2.3 Temperature inversions — the trap
In winter in the northern Bay of Bengal, the surface loses heat to the air and gets cold.
Normally cold surface water would sink and mix. But the fresh lid is so light that it stays on
top even when it is cold.

The result: water gets warmer as you go deeper, for the first few tens of metres. Inversions of
0.5–2 °C are completely normal there.
Why this matters enormously: the intuitive thing to do in the model is to force temperature
to always decrease with depth. We must not do that. It would delete the exact phenomenon
we claim to specialise in. We force profiles to be smooth, never to be always decreasing.

2.4 Depth of the 26 °C water (D26) and heat content (TCHP)
• D26 — how deep you have to go before the water drops below 26 °C. Cyclones cannot run
on water colder than that.
• TCHP — how much heat is stored above that depth. Think of it as how much fuel is in the
tank.
Rough operational numbers: about 50 kJ/cm² supports a cyclone strengthening; 90 and above
is associated with rapid intensification.
In the code: we compute D26 and TCHP from our predicted profiles and show them as maps.
These are the quantities INCOIS and IMD genuinely use, so we are judged on them, not just
on raw temperature error.

2.5 Why surface data can predict the deep ocean
Four inputs, four different jobs:
• Sea surface height (SLA) — the most important one by far. Warm water expands, so a
thick warm layer makes the sea surface bulge. Also, swirling eddies 100–300 km wide push
the thermocline up and down by tens of metres, and that shows up directly in surface
height. This is how the model “sees” downward.
• Sea surface temperature (SST) — tells us about the top of the profile.
• Sea surface salinity (SSS) — tells us about the fresh lid, so it tells us about the barrier
layer. This is the Bay of Bengal-specific input.
• Winds and currents — winds set how hard the ocean is being stirred and push the
thermocline up or down; currents carry water around.
This is not a new idea — the navy has been generating synthetic profiles from surface height
for decades, and there is a 2004 paper doing it with neural networks in the Indian Ocean. Our
contribution is not “ML can do this.” It is: continuous depth, temperature and salinity
together, saltiness-aware physics, and honest uncertainty, in the one basin where saltiness is
the deciding factor.

2.6 Density needs both temperature and salinity
How heavy seawater is depends on temperature and saltiness. You cannot compute it from
temperature alone.
Consequence: if we want to check that our predicted profiles are physically sensible — heavy
water below light water, never the reverse — we are forced to predict salinity too, not just
temperature. This is why our model has two outputs instead of one. It is also why the project
is called Halocline.
In the code: the gsw Python package for all density calculations. It is the community
standard, and using it signals to an ocean scientist that we know what we are doing.

2.7 What we train against, and the honesty problem
We cannot train on Argo floats — there are far too few. So we train on GLORYS, a physics
model that has been nudged into agreement with every observation ever collected. It gives us
a complete ocean, every day, everywhere.
The problem: GLORYS has already absorbed the Argo float data. So when we later “validate
against independent Argo floats”, the floats are not independent — GLORYS already knew
about them, and we learned from GLORYS. Holding floats out of our training does not fix this.
It is baked in.
This is the hardest question the jury will ask. The answer we give is in Part 4. Do not
improvise it.

2.8 The four things we measure
For every depth, against real Argo profiles:
• RMSE — typical size of our error, in °C.
• Bias — are we systematically too warm or too cold.
• Correlation — do we get the ups and downs right.
• Skill score — how much better we are than simply guessing the long-term average for that
place and time of year. This is the one that actually matters. A skill score of 0 means we
added nothing.
Plus the derived ones that decide cyclone behaviour: MLD, BLT, D26, TCHP.

2.9 Two oceans, one model
The Arabian Sea is the opposite of the Bay of Bengal — more evaporation, saltier surface, no
fresh lid, deeper mixing. One model has to handle both.
In the code: every metric is reported separately for the Bay of Bengal and for the Arabian
Sea. If we only report a basin average, we will be asked why, and there is no good answer.

3.1 What goes in and what comes out
In: a small square patch of the ocean surface — 32×32 grid cells, which is 8° × 8°, about 900
km across — with 7 channels stacked (SST, SSS, SLA, two current components, two wind
components), plus the day of the year.
Why a patch instead of a single point: how deep the thermocline sits at one spot depends
on the eddies swirling around that spot. A single-point model throws away that spatial
information, which is most of the signal.
Out: temperature and salinity at any depth you ask for, each with an uncertainty.

3.2 Predict the difference, not the value
Do not ask the network to learn that the tropics are warm. It should not waste capacity on
things we already know.
Instead, we subtract the long-term average for that place and that day of the year — the
climatology — from both the inputs and the target. The network only has to learn the
deviation from normal.
Three benefits:
• Much easier learning problem.
• “Beating the climatology baseline” becomes the literal training objective instead of
something we claim afterwards.
• At 1000 m where there is no signal, the network outputs approximately zero deviation,
which means it falls back gracefully to the climatological average instead of producing
nonsense.

3.3 The depth decoder — our main technical idea
The obvious approach is 15 separate outputs, one per required depth. We do not do that.
Instead the network learns one continuous curve. You hand it a depth — any depth,
including ones it never saw — and it returns the temperature there.
Three reasons this is better, all of which go in the pitch:
1. Smooth, physical profiles. Fifteen independent outputs can disagree with each other and
produce a jagged, physically impossible profile. One curve cannot.
2. We can enforce smoothness directly. Because the curve is continuous, we can
differentiate it and penalise sharp kinks. Fifteen separate outputs give you no derivative to
work with.
3. Any depth, free. The problem statement asks for 15 levels. We can output 500. Good
demo moment.

One catch: neural networks are lazy and prefer to draw gentle curves. Fed raw depth, the
network produces an over-smoothed profile with the thermocline smeared out — exactly the
feature we need. Two standard fixes, both of which we use:
• Fourier features — feed depth in as a set of sine and cosine waves at different frequencies
instead of a single number. This lets the network draw sharp bends.
• Stretched depth axis — feed log(1 + depth/20) instead of raw metres, so the network
spends its capacity near the surface where all the variability is, instead of spreading it
evenly down to 1000 m.

3.4 The loss function — three parts
Part one: CRPS. Our model outputs a mean and an uncertainty. CRPS is a score that rewards
being accurate and being honest about how confident you are. Claiming ±0.1 °C and being
wrong by 2 °C is punished harder than admitting ±2 °C. For a bell-curve prediction it has an
exact formula, so it costs one line of code and no extra compute.
Part two: smoothness. Penalise sharp kinks in the profile. Note again: smooth, not alwaysdecreasing.
Part three: physical sanity. Penalise cases where the model predicts light water sitting
below heavy water, which cannot happen in the real ocean. This needs salinity, which is why
we predict it.
One more detail: temperature varies by several degrees at 30 m and by hundredths of a
degree at 1000 m. If we do not rescale, the loss is entirely dominated by the shallow levels. So
we divide each depth’s error by how much that depth naturally varies. The model then trains
directly on skill score.

3.5 Splitting the data — the mistake that kills projects like this
Today’s ocean looks almost exactly like yesterday’s ocean. If we shuffle all the days randomly
into train and test sets, our test set is effectively sitting inside our training set, and we will
report a beautiful error number that means nothing.
We split by time — train on years up to 2018, validate on 2019, test on 2020–21. And by
region — hold out an entire geographic box the model never sees.
Never randomly.

3.6 Baselines — do not skip these
Our headline number is meaningless on its own. We report it beside:
1. Climatology — just guess the seasonal average. The bar we must clear.
2. Linear regression on SST + SLA + SSS, one per depth.
3. Gradient boosting on the same inputs.
If the deep learning model does not beat #3, we do not have a result. Showing the baselines is
a credibility signal — it tells the jury we tested ourselves honestly. Hiding them tells them we
did not.

3.7 Is the uncertainty honest?
Three checks, all quick:
• Coverage — our stated 95% range should contain about 95% of real observations. If it
contains 60%, we are overconfident.
• Spread vs error — where we say we are unsure, we should actually be more wrong.
• PIT histogram — a flat bar chart means well-calibrated. A U-shape means overconfident.
The best single slide in the whole deck would be an uncertainty map where the river
plumes are visible, because satellite salinity is unreliable there and the model correctly says
so on its own.

Question: “Aren’t you just copying GLORYS?”
The honest answer, in this order:
“Partly, yes, by construction — and here is the table that measures exactly how much.”
Then show one figure. Three rows, compared against the same set of real Argo profiles:
RMSE vs Argo at 100 m
Climatology (guess the average)

worst

Halocline (ours, satellites only)

close to GLORYS

GLORYS (reanalysis, weeks of latency, uses the floats)

best

“GLORYS needs weeks of processing time and the in-situ float data to produce this field. We
produce a comparable field from today’s satellite data alone, with calibrated uncertainty. Here
is how much skill we give up for that, and here is what we gain.”
Then the finishing move: run the model on satellite data from a period after GLORYS
coverage ends, and check it against Argo floats from that period. There is no GLORYS
there at all. That comparison is genuinely clean, and it proves the operational claim directly.
Never say “we beat GLORYS.” It is not true, the jury will know it is not true, and it makes
everything else we say suspect.

The single most important architectural decision
The website never runs the neural network.
Before the demo, the ML team runs the model once over the whole test period and saves
every prediction into a file. The backend just reads that file. This means:
• The map responds instantly instead of in three seconds.
• Nothing can crash during the demo.
• It works with the wifi unplugged.
• The frontend team can build against real data from day 3 without needing a GPU.
We keep one live-inference endpoint, used for exactly one moment in the demo: the near-realtime panel, where we pull today’s satellite data and run the model in front of the jury. That is
the moment that proves it is real. Everything else is precomputed.

The three contracts — agree these on day one
ML → Backend: predictions.zarr with dimensions (time, depth, lat, lon) and variables
t_mean , t_std , s_mean , s_std ; plus metrics.json ; plus model.pt .

Backend → Frontend: a fixed set of endpoints, documented in OpenAPI, plus a fixtures/
folder of hand-written example responses so the frontend can be built before the backend
works.
Everyone → Everyone: a make_fake_data.py that generates all of the above filled with random
numbers in the correct shapes, committed in the first two hours.
This is the trick that lets six people work at once instead of five people waiting for one.

6.1 Design direction
The concept: the page is a water column.
Almost every dashboard is organised left to right. Ours is organised top to bottom, because
depth is the thing the project is about. A vertical depth scale runs down the left side of the
screen — it is both the axis and the control. Drag it, and the map changes depth. The profile
chart on the right uses the same vertical scale with the same tick positions, so your eye can
read straight across the screen at a constant depth. Nobody else will have thought of this, and
it takes ten minutes to explain why it is right.
Palette. Not another dark dashboard with a neon accent. We go the other way: a cool paperwhite ground, like a printed bathymetric chart. Scientific data is printed on white, and the
temperature field — which is vivid orange through blue — is far more striking against paper
than against black.
• Ground #F2F4F5 · Ink #10202B · Rule #C7D0D6 · Accent #0B6E8F · Warning #B5651D
Data colours: use cmocean. thermal for temperature, haline for salinity. These are the
actual colormaps the ocean science community standardised on, and they are perceptually
uniform, meaning equal colour steps represent equal temperature steps. An INCOIS judge will
recognise them instantly. Using matplotlib’s default jet or viridis would mark us as
outsiders. This is a small detail that buys a lot of credibility.
Type. IBM Plex Sans for interface, IBM Plex Mono for all numbers. Plex was designed for
technical interfaces, it is free, and it is not the Inter-everywhere default. Set numbers in
tabular figures so values do not jitter as the depth slider moves.
Motion. One orchestrated moment only: the reveal of the 3D cross-section. Everything else
responds to a click or a drag. No fade-up-on-scroll on every card — that is the generic default
and it reads as unconsidered.

6.3 The five screens
1. Explorer — the layout above. The default view.
2. Cyclone — pick Amphan (May 2020). The storm track animates across the map; below it, a

6.5 How the map layer actually works
We do not build a tile server. The region is fixed, so the backend renders the whole domain as
a single PNG (240 × 100 pixels, about 10 KB) and the frontend drops it onto the map as an
image pinned to fixed corner coordinates. Changing depth means requesting a different PNG.
It is fast, it is about thirty lines of code, and it will not fail on stage.

export.py is the contract with the rest of the team. It should be written in the first week, even

when the model is still bad, so the backend and frontend have something real to work with.
Data volume. Today’s ocean and tomorrow’s ocean are nearly identical, so daily data is
mostly redundant. Take every 5th day, 2010–2019. That is around 19 GB to download and
about 2 GB on disk once processed. Downloading every day for ten years would be several
hundred GB and would teach the model almost nothing extra.
A useful shortcut: GLORYS comes at 1/12° resolution. We need 0.25°. Since 1/12 × 3 = 1/4
exactly, coarsening by a factor of three is all the regridding we ever need — no special
libraries.

PART 11 — THE FIVE-MINUTE DEMO
Rehearse this until it is automatic. One person talks, the others stay quiet.
1. (30s) Open on the map, 18 May 2020, at the surface. “This is what a satellite sees. Thirty
degrees, almost everywhere. It looks the same everywhere.” Point at two spots.
2. (45s) Drag the depth rail down to 100 m. The map transforms. “This is what is actually
there. These two places are completely different, and no satellite can tell you that.”
3. (60s) Click a point. Profile appears with the uncertainty band. Argo dots appear on top of
our curve. “This is a real float measurement. We never saw it in training.”
4. (90s) Cyclone screen. Amphan’s track animates. Cross-section opens. Beside it, the flat
SST line. “Amphan intensified here. Surface temperature does not explain why. The depth
of the warm layer does.”
5. (45s) Validation screen. The three-way table. “We are not claiming to beat the reanalysis.
We are claiming to match it from today’s satellite data alone, without any of the float data it
needs, and here is exactly what that costs.”
6. (30s) Live screen. Press run. Today’s data goes in, a field comes out, timer showing. “That
is real, now, on this laptop.”

PART 12 — THINGS THAT WILL GO WRONG
• Forcing temperature to always decrease with depth. Deletes the Bay of Bengal winter
inversion, which is the phenomenon we are selling.
• Predicting temperature only. Then the physics check is impossible and the project name
is a lie.
• Shuffling the data randomly. Gives a beautiful, fictional error number.
• Computing the climatology using all years. Leaks the test set into training.
• Forgetting the land mask. Predicting 1000 m temperatures in the Sundarbans on a live
map is a bad thirty seconds.
• Training on model salinity and running on satellite salinity. The most likely cause of
something that validates beautifully and fails on stage.
• Running the neural network during the demo. Precompute. Always.
• Relying on venue wifi. Everything offline, plus the video.
• Six people on one branch. Predictable and avoidable.
• Hiding the baselines. Judges assume the worst about numbers you did not show.
