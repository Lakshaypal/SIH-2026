PART 1 — THE SCIENTIFIC FOUNDATION

PART 1 — THE SCIENTIFIC FOUNDATION

1.1 What exactly are we solving?

Satellites continuously observe the surface of the ocean, but they cannot directly observe the temperature hundreds of metres below it. Yet the subsurface state affects:

  • Cyclone intensification — warm subsurface reservoirs fuel rapid intensification
  • Ocean heat content — drives global climate variability
  • Marine heatwaves — threaten ecosystems and fisheries
  • Stratification and circulation — govern nutrient transport and biological productivity
  • Ocean forecasting — subsurface initialisation is critical for numerical prediction

Direct instruments such as ARGO provide valuable vertical profiles but are too sparse — roughly one profile per 3° every 10 days — to provide a continuous daily 0.25° field. The central idea is therefore:

surface observations → learn hidden subsurface state → produce a continuous 3-D temperature field

The scientific basis is that the subsurface is not independent of the surface. Thermocline displacement, mesoscale eddies, wind-driven mixing, surface salinity and surface currents all leave observable signatures at the surface.

The system produces a daily 0.25° subsurface temperature field at the 15 depths specified by INCOIS: 0, 5, 10, 20, 30, 50, 75, 100, 125, 150, 200, 300, 500, 700 and 1000 m.

The official problem requires the North Indian Ocean domain 5°N–30°N, 45°E–105°E, daily 0.25° output, surface SST/SSS/SSH-SLA/currents/winds as input, GLORYS temperature as the training target and ARGO as independent validation.

1.2 The key scientific insight — surface signatures reveal subsurface state

The network should not be presented as "CNN sees seven maps and guesses 15 temperatures." Instead, present it as:

"We infer the hidden ocean state from its surface expression."

The major physical pathways are illustrated below:

Each surface variable carries specific information about the hidden ocean state:

Surface Variable Physical Information Carried
SST Surface heat state, upper-ocean thermal constraint
SSS Density / halocline / barrier layer — controls stratification in BoB
SLA / SSH Thermocline displacement, eddy structure — most informative predictor
Surface currents Water-mass transport, advective information
Surface winds Mixing energy, Ekman pumping, thermocline displacement

This is strongly aligned with physical oceanography: SLA contains information about thermocline displacement; SST constrains the upper-ocean thermal state; SSS influences the halocline and barrier layer; winds influence mixing/Ekman transport; and currents carry water-mass information.

1.3 Why SLA is especially important

A particularly strong part of the scientific pitch is sea-level anomaly (SLA).

SSH has a mass component and a steric component. The steric part is the depth-integrated density anomaly. Since density anomalies in the tropics are dominated by thermocline displacement, SLA is essentially a remote measurement of how deep the thermocline is. It is the single most informative predictor.

Mesoscale ocean eddies (100–300 km, the dominant variability) displace the thermocline by tens of metres and show up directly in SLA:

  • Anticyclonic (warm) eddy → positive SLA → depressed thermocline → anomalously warm subsurface
  • Cyclonic (cold) eddy → negative SLA → elevated thermocline → anomalously cool subsurface

SLA provides an indirect measurement of the vertical position of subsurface thermal structure. The steric component of sea-level anomaly is linked to integrated density structure and thermocline displacement.

This gives an excellent judge-facing line:

"We are not asking AI to hallucinate the deep ocean. We are learning the physical surface signatures that reveal where the thermocline has moved."

1.4 Why SSS is critical in the Bay of Bengal

The most distinctive feature of the North Indian Ocean is that temperature alone does not determine stratification.

The Bay of Bengal receives strong freshwater input from precipitation and major rivers (Ganges–Brahmaputra, Irrawaddy). This creates strong surface freshening and a halocline — a sharp vertical salinity gradient.

That can produce:

fresh surface layer → salinity-driven density stratification → barrier layer → reduced vertical mixing

This is important for cyclone physics because a deep warm layer can remain beneath the surface even when SST alone looks similar to another location.

Key numbers: - Thermal expansion coefficient α ≈ 2–3 × 10⁻⁴ per °C - Haline contraction coefficient β ≈ 7.6 × 10⁻⁴ per psu - 1 psu of salinity ≈ 3 °C in density terms — in the BoB, salinity, not temperature, controls stratification

This is why SSS is a particularly important predictor, and why the model must be salinity-aware even though the official target is temperature.

1.5 Bay of Bengal vs Arabian Sea — two opposite regimes

The North Indian Ocean domain covers two fundamentally different oceanographic regimes. A single model must handle both. Report metrics separately for BoB and Arabian Sea — if you only report a basin mean you will be asked why, and you won't have an answer.

Feature Bay of Bengal Arabian Sea
Freshwater Excess precipitation + rivers → fresh surface Evaporation > precipitation → saltier surface
Stratification driver Salinity (halocline) Temperature (thermocline)
Barrier layer Thick, suppresses entrainment Absent in most of the basin
Mixed layer Shallow, 10–30 m Deep, 50–120 m
Temperature inversions Common in winter BoB (0.5–2 °C) Rare
Key features River plumes, cyclone barrier layers Monsoon upwelling off Somalia/Oman

This gives us a built-in experiment:

  • Does adding SSS improve reconstruction more strongly in the Bay of Bengal?
  • Does adding surface currents improve reconstruction particularly in the Arabian Sea / monsoon regime?

These become genuine empirical research questions — not fake architectural novelties.

PART 2 — ARCHITECTURE

PART 2 — ARCHITECTURE

2.1 Final architecture overview

The backbone deliberately follows a U-Net architecture with an explicit bottleneck embedding. It is the best compromise between spatial modelling, compute and hackathon reliability.

The architecture has five major stages:

  1. Input harmonisation → common 0.25° daily grid
  2. Dual-branch feature extraction → raw observations + physics-derived features
  3. Physics-aware encoder → U-Net CNN with skip connections
  4. Satellite Ocean Embedding → bottleneck + lightweight attention
  5. Multi-head decoder → temperature, auxiliary salinity, uncertainty

2.2 Input layer — the ten channels

The official seven surface variables are:

# Variable Source
1 SST NOAA OISST v2.1
2 SSS SMAP L3 (RSS or JPL)
3 SLA / SSH CMEMS SEALEVEL_GLO_PHY_L4
4 Surface current U OSCAR v2
5 Surface current V OSCAR v2
6 Surface wind U CCMP v3.0
7 Surface wind V CCMP v3.0

The model additionally receives:

# Channel Purpose
8 Ocean/land mask Prevents prediction over land
9 Observation-validity mask Distinguishes measurements from interpolation
10 Day-of-year encoding (sin/cos) Captures seasonal context

The use of validity masks is important because satellite datasets contain missing/gap-filled regions, and the network should know whether a value represents a measurement or an interpolation.

2.3 Physics Feature Branch

This is where OceanEmbed-PG becomes distinctive. We retain the seven official variables, but derive inexpensive deterministic physical features from them.

Feature Formula Physical Meaning
Wind speed |W| = √(U²_w + V²_w) Magnitude of atmospheric forcing
Wind stress τ = ρ_air · C_D · |W| · W Surface momentum forcing on the ocean
Wind-stress curl ∇×τ = ∂τ_y/∂x − ∂τ_x/∂y Ekman pumping → thermocline displacement
Current magnitude |U_c| = √(U²_c + V²_c) Transport intensity
SLA gradients ∂SLA/∂x, ∂SLA/∂y Spatially organized eddy structures

Key point: These are not new observational inputs. They are deterministic physics-informed transformations of the officially provided inputs. We do not need a sophisticated atmospheric model — the purpose is simply to transform raw variables into features directly related to ocean dynamics.

Wind-stress curl is particularly useful: it contributes to Ekman pumping and therefore vertical displacement of the thermocline. The underlying mechanism is: wind stress / Ekman forcing changes thermocline depth and upper-ocean mixing.

2.4 The Satellite Ocean Embedding

The raw input is a stack of maps: 10 × 100 × 240. The network transforms this into a lower-dimensional representation:

surface observations → encoder → latent Z

We call Z the Satellite Ocean Embedding.

This embedding is intended to capture latent properties such as: - Eddy structure - Thermocline displacement - Surface stratification - Mixing regime - Water-mass transport - Monsoon state

This directly satisfies the PS's explicit "satellite embedding engine" requirement, while making the embedding an inspectable intermediate output. The recommended approach is an explicit bottleneck rather than an opaque end-to-end model.

2.5 Why U-Net?

U-Net provides two critical advantages:

1. Hierarchical abstraction through the encoder:

surface maps → local patterns → eddy structures → regional ocean state → embedding

2. Skip connections retain high-resolution spatial information.

This is important because the final output must also be spatially resolved: 15 depths × 100 × 240, rather than one scalar.

U-Net is the recommended practical architecture because the domain is only around 100×240 cells at the requested resolution, making full-grid dense prediction tractable. A full Vision Transformer is unnecessarily data- and compute-hungry for the hackathon.

2.6 Lightweight attention at the bottleneck

At the U-Net bottleneck, we add a small self-attention block:

CNN encoder → embedding → small self-attention block → decoder

The purpose is to give the embedding some ability to relate distant spatial features. For example, western Bay of Bengal mesoscale patterns can influence eastern Bay of Bengal patterns through this mechanism.

This is preferable to a full ViT, which is considered unnecessarily data- and compute-hungry for the hackathon.

2.7 Temperature decoder — 15 depths

The main decoder outputs T̂(z, x, y) for the 15 standard depths:

z = 0, 5, 10, 20, 30, 50, 75, 100, 125, 150, 200, 300, 500, 700, 1000 m

The final output shape is: 15 × H × W, where each channel corresponds to a depth.

This is intentionally simpler than a full continuous-depth neural field. The PS explicitly asks for those 15 standard depths, and a 15-channel output is easier to train, validate and demonstrate within a hackathon.

2.8 Auxiliary salinity decoder

This is the main physics-guided modification.

The official target remains temperature. But internally we also predict Ŝ(z, x, y) as an auxiliary task:

Embedding
    │
    ├────► Temperature decoder → T(z)    [primary output]
    │
    └────► Salinity decoder → S(z)       [auxiliary internal target]

Why? Because seawater density is approximately ρ = ρ(T, S, p), and therefore the vertical density structure depends jointly on temperature and salinity.

This is especially important in the Bay of Bengal, where salinity strongly affects stratification. Co-predicting salinity enables density-based physical constraints that would otherwise be impossible.

This gives a very good judge explanation:

"We don't force physics onto temperature in isolation. We reconstruct the temperature–salinity state that actually determines ocean stratification."

Salinity is an auxiliary internal target; the required final output is still temperature.

2.9 Anomaly learning

The model should not primarily learn absolute temperature. Instead:

T' = T − T_clim(doy, x, y)

where T_clim is the day-of-year climatology. Then:

GLORYS temperature → seasonal climatology subtraction → temperature anomaly → model → predicted anomaly → + climatology → final temperature

This removes much of the predictable seasonal background and makes the model focus on dynamically meaningful anomalies. The network then learns deviations from the normal state and naturally falls back toward zero anomaly where surface information is weak.

Critical: Compute climatology from the training years only — never from test years. That is leakage.

PART 3 — PHYSICS-GUIDED LOSS

PART 3 — PHYSICS-GUIDED LOSS

This is the central differentiator. Instead of a plain MSE loss, we use a composite physics-guided objective:

The complete loss structure is:

$$L = L_{temp} + \lambda_1 L_{smooth} + \lambda_2 L_{density} + \lambda_3 L_{salinity} + \lambda_4 L_{D26}$$

Component What it enforces
L_temp Accurate temperature reconstruction (depth-weighted Huber)
L_smooth Physically smooth vertical profiles
L_density Gravitationally stable density structure
L_salinity Consistent T–S relationship for density constraints
L_D26 Cyclone-relevant isotherm depth accuracy

This is what makes the model physics-guided rather than merely physics-themed.

3.1 Temperature reconstruction loss

Use Huber loss rather than pure MSE:

L_temp = Huber(T̂', T')

Why Huber? Ocean data contains noisy/outlier values and Huber behaves like: - MSE for small errors → precise near zero - MAE-like for large errors → robust to outliers

Huber is a practical default for noisy geophysical targets.

OceanEmbed-PG · SIH26066

PART 3 — PHYSICS-GUIDED LOSS

3.2 Thermocline-aware depth weighting

Not every depth matters equally. The thermocline (20–200 m) is much harder than the deep ocean because a small depth error produces a large temperature error. Therefore:

L_temp = Σ_d  w_d · Huber(T̂_d, T_d)

where w_d is determined from training climatological variability. We deliberately avoid allowing the easiest deep levels to dominate the headline performance.

3.3 Vertical smoothness loss

The predicted ocean profile should be physically smooth. Using finite differences:

L_smooth = (1/(D−2)) · Σ_d  (T̂_{d+1} − 2·T̂_d + T̂_{d−1})²

This penalises unrealistic oscillations (e.g. 27 → 26.8 → 27.5 → 24 → 26.9) but does not force the temperature to always decrease monotonically. This distinction matters.

3.4 Why NOT to impose monotonic temperature

A simplistic solution might enforce T(0) ≥ T(5) ≥ T(10) ≥ … We must not hard-code that.

The Bay of Bengal can exhibit temperature inversions in which temperature temporarily increases with depth within a stratified upper layer. In winter (roughly Dec–Feb) in the northern BoB, the surface loses heat to the atmosphere but the fresh cap prevents convective overturning — inversions of ~0.5–2 °C are routine.

Imposing temperature monotonicity would delete the exact phenomenon that makes the Bay of Bengal scientifically interesting.

Therefore: - ✅ Smoothness = yes - ❌ Blind temperature monotonicity = no

3.5 Density stability loss

This is the strongest physics constraint. For predicted temperature and salinity:

ρ̂(z) = ρ(T̂(z), Ŝ(z), p(z))

Under normal stable stratification, density should generally increase with depth. For adjacent levels:

L_density = Σ_d  ReLU(ρ̂_d − ρ̂_{d+1})

If ρ(100m) > ρ(125m) → penalised (unstable profile) If ρ(100m) < ρ(125m) → no penalty (physically stable)

Implementation: - Version 1: Use gsw (NumPy, not differentiable) for diagnostics and validation - Version 2: Precompute TEOS-10 coefficients / α(T,S,p) and β(T,S,p) on a coarse lookup grid with gsw once, then interpolate that table differentiably inside PyTorch

This is an excellent hackathon compromise — honest, cheap, and defensible.

3.6 D26 auxiliary objective

D26 is the depth where T(z) = 26 °C. Instead of treating this only as a final graph, we can make it one of our operational objectives.

From the true temperature profile, interpolate the depth where T = 26 °C to create a D26 target. The bottleneck has an auxiliary small head:

embedding → D26 prediction

And we add:

L_D26 = Huber(D26_predicted, D26_target)

This forces the embedding to preserve information relevant to cyclone heat content. This is not required by the PS, so it should be described as our proposed operational extension.

3.7 Final composite loss

The complete practical loss:

$$L_{total} = L_{Huber-T} + 0.05 \cdot L_{smooth} + 0.05 \cdot L_{density} + 0.10 \cdot L_{D26} + 0.10 \cdot L_{Huber-S}$$

The exact coefficients should be tuned experimentally; these are initial engineering values, not scientifically established constants. The important concept is the structure:

Temperature accuracy
       +
Profile smoothness
       +
Density stability
       +
Salinity consistency
       +
Cyclone-relevant D26 accuracy

PART 4 — UNCERTAINTY

PART 4 — UNCERTAINTY

The final model should optionally estimate T̂(z) ± σ(z), but we don't make complicated probabilistic modelling a prerequisite.

MC Dropout — the practical approach

Keep dropout active at inference. Run N forward passes:

prediction_1, prediction_2, ..., prediction_N

And calculate:

σ(z) = Std(T̂_1, T̂_2, …, T̂_N)

This costs little because the architecture doesn't need to change.

Observation-aware uncertainty

An especially useful addition: uncertainty should be higher where input quality is lower.

missing / interpolated SSS → validity mask → model knows confidence is lower → higher uncertainty

This is particularly relevant around the Bay of Bengal river-plume / coastal regions where satellite salinity is noisy.

Degraded-SSS augmentation

During training, randomly corrupt SSS to simulate real satellite uncertainty:

true SSS → noise → smoothing → random missing pixels → model input

This prevents the most common failure mode: train/serve skew on SSS. Training on GLORYS SSS and deploying on SMAP SSS creates a mismatch that will cause the model to fail in the demo.

Notes that will save you time:

  • OISST over OSTIA. OISST is already 0.25°, daily, and needs no authentication. Use it for the hackathon.

  • Drop altimetric geostrophic currents, or use OSCAR instead. Geostrophic U,V from the SLA product are derived from SLA — they are collinear with an input you already have and add almost no information. OSCAR includes the Ekman component, which is genuinely new information.

  • SSS is your weak link. SMAP is ~40 km footprint, noisy, degraded by land contamination and radio-frequency interference — worst exactly in the river-plume region that matters most. If you train on GLORYS SSS and deploy on SMAP SSS you have train/serve skew and the model will fail in the demo. Either train on SMAP directly, or train on GLORYS SSS with noise-and-smoothing augmentation that mimics SMAP's error characteristics.

  • Bathymetry mask. Do not let the model predict 1000 m temperature where the ocean is 200 m deep. Mask each depth level by bathymetry and exclude those cells from both loss and metrics. Easy to forget, embarrassing on a map.

  • Grid alignment. GLORYS at 1/12° coarsened by a factor of 3 gives exactly 0.25°, and the cell centres land on the same 0.125°-offset grid as OISST and the CMEMS L4 altimetry products. Verify this once.

Data volume — do the arithmetic before you start downloading

Region 5–30 °N, 45–105 °E is 300 × 720 cells at 1/12°, or 100 × 240 at 0.25°.

Naively downloading GLORYS daily for 10 years at native resolution with all depth levels is several hundred GB. You cannot do that in a hackathon.

The fix: subsample in time. Subsurface ocean state decorrelates over roughly 10–30 days at mesoscale. Consecutive daily fields are nearly redundant. Take every 5th day.

2010–2019, every 5th day = ~730 fields
Requesting only ~15 depth levels, T and S:
730 × 300 × 720 × 15 × 2 × 4 bytes ≈ 19 GB transferred
→ ~2 GB as float32 zarr after coarsening to 0.25°

That is tractable. Use copernicusmarine.open_dataset(), which returns a lazy xarray object backed by cloud zarr — subset, coarsen, and compute so only the needed chunks ever cross the network.

Target generation
GLORYS → North Indian Ocean crop → 0.25° regridding/coarsening → 15 target depths
→ day-of-year climatology → T anomaly

GLORYS at 1/12° can be coarsened by a factor of three to 0.25°. Process incrementally rather than downloading everything at native resolution.

PART 6 — VALIDATION

PART 6 — VALIDATION

Training split — never randomly shuffle

Adjacent days of ocean state are nearly identical. A random split lets the model effectively see the same phenomenon in training and testing. Random splits are severe leakage.

The recommended strategy:

Split Period
TRAIN Earlier years (e.g. 2010–2018)
VALIDATION Later year (e.g. 2019)
TEST Latest 1–2 years (e.g. 2020–2021)

Within training, use blocked spatial validation rather than random pixels.

The evaluation pipeline
MODEL → predicted profile → match with independent ARGO →
    RMSE, MAE, Bias, Correlation, R²

Separately by: - Region: Bay of Bengal vs Arabian Sea - Season: Summer, monsoon, winter, etc. - Depth: per-level reporting

The most important scientific validation — three-way comparison
                    WITHHELD ARGO
                         │
              ┌──────────┼──────────┐
              ↓          ↓          ↓
          OceanEmbed   GLORYS   Climatology
              ↓          ↓          ↓
            RMSE        RMSE       RMSE

This prevents the weak claim "Our model matches GLORYS." GLORYS supplied the training targets, so that comparison alone is insufficient.

Instead, we ask: How well does our predicted field reproduce an independently observed ARGO profile?

The three-way comparison is one of the most important validation figures.

GLORYS is not ground truth

This needs to be explicitly stated in the pitch.

GLORYS = ocean model + data assimilation + observations

Therefore: training target ≠ perfect truth. ARGO is used as the independent physical observation check.

The framing to give the jury. Not "we beat GLORYS". Instead:

"GLORYS needs weeks of latency and in-situ assimilation to produce this field. OceanEmbed-PG produces a comparable field from same-day satellite data alone, with calibrated uncertainty, and here is exactly how much skill we give up for it."

That survives cross-examination. The other framing does not.

PART 7 — OPERATIONAL PRODUCTS

PART 7 — OPERATIONAL PRODUCTS

The model does not stop at temperature. From the predicted profile we calculate:

D26 — Depth of the 26 °C isotherm
T(D26) = 26 °C

Below 26 °C the ocean cannot sustain a tropical cyclone.

TCHP — Tropical Cyclone Heat Potential
TCHP = ρ · c_p · ∫₀^D26 (T(z) − 26) dz

with ρ ≈ 1026 kg/m³, c_p ≈ 3993 J/(kg·K). Reported in kJ/cm².

Rough operational thresholds: ~50 kJ/cm² supports intensification, ~90+ is associated with rapid intensification. Rapid intensification is ≥30 kt increase in maximum sustained wind in 24 h.

Why this is more useful than an SST-only system

Consider:

Location SST D26 Interpretation
A 30 °C 45 m Shallow warm layer — storm may self-limit
B 30 °C 120 m Deep warm reservoir — rapid intensification risk

From the satellite's SST map: A ≈ B. Both look like 30 °C from space.

But our model reveals: - A → shallow warm layer, cyclone mixing brings up cold water, storm weakens - B → deep warm reservoir, mixing entrains warm water, storm intensifies explosively

SST alone cannot distinguish these two cases. That single sentence is the entire problem statement.

PART 10 — THINGS THAT WILL GO WRONG

PART 10 — THINGS THAT WILL GO WRONG

  • Random train/test split. Adjacent days are near-identical. You will get an RMSE of 0.1 °C and it will be fiction. Split by time and by region only.

  • Climatology computed over all years. Leakage. Compute it from the training years alone.

  • Monotonicity constraint. Deletes the BoB winter inversion, which is the phenomenon you are selling.

  • Density penalty without salinity. Physically impossible. Co-predict S.

  • Land and shallow water. Mask by bathymetry per level or your maps will show 1000 m temperatures in the Sundarbans.

  • Gridded Argo from the LAS as your validation set. It is an objective analysis — smoothed, and derived from the same floats. Use raw delayed-mode profiles for the honest test; use the gridded product for maps only.

  • Train/serve skew on SSS. Training on reanalysis salinity and deploying on satellite salinity is the most likely cause of a demo that looks great in validation and terrible live.

  • Six people, one branch. Predictable. One branch per person, PRs into main, no direct pushes.

  • Live demo over conference wifi. Have the offline version and the video.

  • GLORYS called "ground truth." It is not. It is a reanalysis. The jury from INCOIS will know this. Frame carefully.

PART 11 — THE FIVE QUESTIONS THE JURY WILL ASK

PART 11 — THE FIVE QUESTIONS THE JURY WILL ASK

Rehearse these until the answers are automatic. One person answers, the rest stay silent.

1. "Aren't you just emulating GLORYS?"

Partly, yes, by construction, and here is the three-way table quantifying it. The contribution is producing a comparable field from same-day satellite data with no in-situ assimilation and weeks less latency, with calibrated uncertainty. Then show the NRT result.

2. "How is this different from Argo interpolation?"

Argo gives roughly one profile per 3° every 10 days. Most of the basin is unobserved at any moment. We produce a daily 0.25° field with uncertainty that widens where the surface constraint is weak.

3. "Your 1000 m numbers look like climatology."

They are, and that is physics: surface fields carry almost no information below the main thermocline. That is why we report a per-depth skill score, and why our uncertainty widens correctly with depth. Then show the spread–skill plot.

4. "Why not just use SST?"

Show the Amphan case. Two locations, same SST, different D26, different storm outcome. SST alone cannot distinguish a shallow warm layer from a deep warm reservoir.

5. "What happens when SMAP salinity is bad near the coast?"

Uncertainty widens there because we trained with degraded-SSS augmentation. Show the σ map with the river plume visible in it. If your model does this, it is your best single slide.

The novelty claim

Don't claim "We invented a new U-Net." Instead:

"We introduce a physics-guided satellite embedding pipeline tailored to the North Indian Ocean that explicitly separates the physical pathways controlling subsurface structure — thermocline displacement from SLA, mixing from winds, advection from currents, and salinity-driven stratification from SSS — and validates their contribution through regional ablation against independent ARGO observations."

That is a credible innovation statement. It is system-level scientific innovation + regional empirical evidence — the defensible novelty territory.
